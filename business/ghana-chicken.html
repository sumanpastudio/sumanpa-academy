From 4,000 to 253,000 Tonnes: Can Ghana Grow Its Own Chicken Again?

24 November 2026 Agriculture

Roughly four in five chickens eaten in Ghana are imported. Feed costs are the reason, and also the opportunity.

In 1998, Ghana imported about 4,000 tonnes of chicken, according to the minister. By 2004 it was 104,000 tonnes. By 2022, USDA estimates put imports at about 253,000 tonnes. Today, according to USDA figures, roughly four in five of the chickens Ghanaians eat come from abroad, mostly as cut parts from countries including Belgium, the United States, Brazil, Poland and the Netherlands. The minister has gone further, saying that more than 95 percent of the chicken consumed in Ghana is imported. This article uses the lower USDA figure, which can be checked against the published report.

Ghana has farmers, land and a population that loves chicken. So why is the market dominated by frozen birds from thousands of kilometres away? The answer is not a lack of effort on local farms. It starts in the feed bag. This article looks at what it would take to reverse the trend, and why the farmer who solves the feed problem may be sitting on one of the better investment opportunities in Ghanaian agriculture.

Why local chicken costs more: feed

Feed makes up roughly 70 percent of the cost of raising a chicken, according to a Ghanaian research institute and the national poultry farmers’ association, so the price of a bird is largely the price of what it eats. For a processed local bird, feed alone is about 42 percent of the selling price. A typical ration is built on two crops: maize and soybean meal.

Drought has made feed supply less certain. In August 2024 the government banned exports of maize, rice and soybeans to protect domestic supply after dry spells hit eight regions.

The price gap at the counter

Walk into a Ghanaian market and the choice is rarely about taste. It is about price. In figures from late 2024, a processed 2.0 kg bird from a local farm sold for GH¢96.70, which works out to roughly GH¢49 per kg. Imported broiler (chicken raised specifically for meat production) averaged about GH¢40 per kg. That puts local chicken about a fifth more expensive. A 2023 University of Ghana study of markets in four regions put the imported discount higher, at 27 to 30 percent, even after a 35 percent increase in tariffs.

The cost breakdown shows why. For that 2.0 kg bird, feed alone comes to about GH¢40.50, around 42 percent of the selling price. Processing, packaging and cold storage add more, and the rest has to cover chicks, vaccines, labour, transport and the farmer’s margin.

Efficiency matters too. In one Ghanaian study, the research institute’s own flock needed 1.56 kg of feed per kg of live weight, while other farmers needed 2.12 kg. That is about 36 percent more feed for the same bird. Research comparing typical Ghanaian broiler farms with European ones found production costs that are well above German and Dutch levels.

The loop. Feed is expensive because key ingredients are imported. Local farmers who pay those prices cannot match the shelf price of imported frozen chicken. When they cannot meet demand at that price, the gap is filled by imports. Each step reinforces the next.

What it would take to grow local production

Replacing imported chicken is not a single problem. It is four, and each one depends on the others.

  1. Raise efficiency on the farm. The average farm uses about 36 percent more feed per bird than the research institute’s own flock. Better breeds, housing and feeding practice would lower the cost of every bird.
  2. Build a steadier feed supply. Maize and soybean meal are the main feed crops, so a poultry business that depends on them needs buyers who sign contracts ahead of planting, storage that keeps grain through the lean months, and prices that stay predictable from year to year.
  3. Fix processing and the cold chain. Frozen imports arrive packed, priced and ready to store. A local bird has to be slaughtered, dressed, packaged and kept cold. In the Graphic article’s figures, processing costs GH¢6 per bird, packaging GH¢24 per box and cold storage GH¢1 per bird. Cold transport adds more, at about GH¢3,500 per 5 tonnes when farmers share a vehicle. Shared processing and cold storage, where farmers split these costs, would help most because small farms cannot afford them alone.
  4. Shorten the chain between farm and table. Every step between the farm and the counter adds cost, from processing and packaging to cold storage and transport. Fewer handovers and shared facilities would leave more of the price with the farmer.

The honest summary is this: local chicken can compete only when feed costs fall, and feed costs fall only when yields, storage and supply contracts improve together.

Where the opportunity is, and where it is not

The market is large and already proven. Ghanaians buy hundreds of thousands of tonnes of chicken a year, and roughly four in five birds are imported. Any local producer who can sell at a competitive price is not looking for demand. The demand is there.

The difficulty is the price. Local chicken costs about GH¢9 more per kg than imported, and for a typical local bird feed alone is about 42 percent of the selling price. That means the opportunity is not simply “start a poultry farm.” It is to lower the cost of what goes into the bird. There are four places to look:

  • Feed supply. Buying maize and soybeans directly from farmers under contract, storing them, and milling feed can reduce the price volatility that hurts feed buyers now.
  • Farm efficiency. A flock that needs 1.56 kg of feed per kg of weight instead of 2.12 saves roughly a third of its feed bill.
  • Shared processing and cold storage. Small farms struggle to afford these alone. A facility that serves many of them could earn fees while lowering their costs.
  • The distribution chain. Every handover between farm and counter adds cost, so a business that connects the two for a fair margin has room to work.

The risks

An honest reading of the numbers shows how thin the margins are. On a bird that sells for GH¢96.70, feed alone costs GH¢40.50. The rest must pay for processing, packaging, cold storage, chicks, vaccines, labour, transport and the farmer’s profit. Reliable figures for most of those items were not available, so no one should assume a profit until they have been checked.

  • Import competition. Frozen imported chicken arrives at a lower price, and a local producer must match it or sell on freshness and quality.
  • Weather. A poor harvest can tighten feed grain supply quickly.
  • Policy changes. Rules on grain and chicken trade can change with little warning.
  • Currency. Imported chicken and feed ingredients are priced abroad, so exchange rate moves affect the comparison between local and imported prices.
  • Thin evidence. Several figures in this article come from single sources or are dated. Anyone planning to invest should test them against current prices and a real farm’s records.

Questions worth asking before putting money in

  1. What does a chick, a vaccine programme and a month of labour cost today, and what is left after those?
  2. Can the farm or mill secure maize and soybeans under contract at a price that holds through a bad harvest?
  3. Is there reliable cold storage and transport within reach, and who pays for it?
  4. Who will buy the birds, at what price, and how quickly do they pay?

Closing

Ghana did not become a chicken importer overnight, and it will not stop being one overnight. The slide from 4,000 tonnes in 1998 to about 253,000 tonnes in 2022 took more than two decades, and it followed the cost of feed at every step.

But the same numbers point the other way. Feed is the largest cost in the business, so it is also the place where the right change counts most. Better yields, steadier grain supply, shared cold storage and a shorter path from farm to table are not exotic ideas. They are practical, and each one lowers the cost of a bird.

For Ghanaians at home and abroad, that makes this more than a story about imports. It is a question about where we put our money, our skills and our attention. The demand is already there. What it needs now is someone who can solve the feed problem.
