China Says It Will Help Build Ghana’s “New Economy.” Here Is What to Watch.

An offer is not a deal. The details will decide who benefits.

26 November 2026 China

On Monday, November 16, China’s ambassador to Ghana, Cong Song, told reporters in Accra that Beijing is ready to support Ghana’s New Economy Programme. He named artificial intelligence (AI), green technology, industrial upgrading, green agriculture, and sustainable mining as the areas with the most potential. He also said China wants cooperation to move beyond extracting and exporting raw materials toward local processing and value addition.

It made headlines across Ghanaian media. But read closely, it is an offer of partnership, not an announcement of money. Here is what is actually on the table, and what readers should ask next.

What Ghana is building

The ambassador’s remarks came days after Finance Minister Ato Forson unveiled the New Economy Programme in the 2027 Budget on November 12. Our deep dive on this is coming up. It is a US$10 billion, four-year plan running from 2027 to 2030, with GH¢30 billion set aside for 2027 (about 1.5% of GDP). Reuters reported that it is domestically funded.

In practice, the money comes mainly from lowering the government’s primary surplus target by 1 percentage point of GDP, plus about 0.5% of GDP from tax reforms already passed. A US$500 million World Bank financing for oil palm is also proposed, with Parliament due to consider it, and the private sector is expected to carry “principal responsibility” for investment. So, there is no obvious funding gap for China to fill. The opening is expertise and, potentially, private investment.

The plan has eight pillars. These are the ones most relevant to this story:

  • Critical minerals value addition (US$1.5 billion). The goal is to refine 70% of artisanal and small-scale gold locally by December 2027 and all of it by December 2028, when raw gold exports from that sector are to be prohibited. Half of manganese and bauxite is to be processed locally by the end of 2028 and all of it by 2030. A Western Region minerals enclave and the Takoradi to Nyinahin railway are due to start from 2027.
  • Commercial agriculture (US$3 billion). This includes raising domestic cocoa processing to at least 50% by 2028 and 100% by 2030.
  • Technology economy (US$300 million). This funds a National AI Centre, rural 4G upgrades and digital skills training for young people.
  • Energy, tourism, textiles and pharmaceuticals make up the rest.

What China is offering

The ambassador said the two countries could align China’s 15th Five-Year Plan (2026 to 2030) with Ghana’s 24-Hour+ Economy and Reset Ghana agenda. He pointed to Ghana’s National AI Strategy and One Million Coders Programme as natural starting points for cooperation, and said China is prepared to share its development experience and transfer suitable technologies.

He backed this with Chinese figures with an AI industry valued at more than 1.2 trillion yuan in 2025, and about 16.6 million electric vehicles produced that year. He urged Chinese EV makers operating in Ghana to invest in maintenance facilities and technical training, not only vehicle sales, and noted early talks with Ghanaian institutions on EV training.

What reports of the briefing do not mention is any specific funding commitment.

Where the offer meets Ghana’s plan

China has attached no figures to its offer, but the amounts below are Ghana’s own budget allocations. They show where the areas China named line up with funded pillars, not what China will contribute. The matching is ours.

The mining promise meets the mining record

Value addition is where the two agendas line up most closely. If Chinese partners bring processing technology and training, Ghana’s gold, manganese and bauxite targets become more realistic.

But mining is also where trust is thinnest. Ghana and China have been in a diplomatic row over illegal gold mining, known as galamsey, which has damaged rivers, forests and farmland. The ambassador has rejected the idea that Chinese nationals are solely to blame. One LSE analysis argues that the recurring galamsey problem is largely a result of Ghanaian collusion combined with Chinese migrant involvement. A partnership on “sustainable mining” will be judged less by what is announced, and more by whether the rivers recover.

Partner or dependent?

Supporters point to the numbers. China is Ghana’s largest bilateral creditor, but a 2024 World Bank report put Chinese creditors at about 7% of Ghana’s public external debt, far below the share in countries like Zambia. China also co-chaired the creditor committee that delivered Ghana’s US$2.8 billion debt relief deal.

Ghana also negotiates from firmer ground than in 2022. Annual average inflation was 4.6% at the end of October, and the IMF has backed the budget’s lower surplus target to leave room for growth spending. Debt stood at 47.5% of GDP, though all three major rating agencies still rate Ghana below investment grade.

Skeptics counter that China still accounts for roughly 30% of what Ghana owes other governments, and that resource-backed loans have been part of past arrangements. A prominent Ghanaian scholar described the wider Africa-China relationship as “heavy on form and very light on substance.” Questions also remain over how the New Economy itself will be financed.

A note on the numbers: figures vary by definition and by who is reporting them. China’s previous ambassador put official debt at 3.6% of Ghana’s external debt, while the share of bilateral debt is far higher. We have not found a post-2025 breakdown from Ghana’s Ministry of Finance.

What it could mean for Ghanaians abroad

This is our read, not reported fact. Several pillars create room for diaspora skills and capital:

  • Technology. A National AI Centre and digital skills programmes need trainers, engineers and product people. Diaspora professionals are natural partners.
  • Processing and manufacturing. Cocoa, gold, textiles and pharmaceuticals all need technical expertise, quality standards and export networks.
  • Green mobility. If EV makers do invest in training and maintenance, there is a skills gap that diaspora technicians and trainers could help fill.
  • Pharmaceuticals. Ghana imports 70 to 75% of its medicines, at a cost of about US$500 million a year. The plan aims to make 60 to 80% locally by 2030, with roughly 20,000 jobs. That calls for pharmacists, quality specialists and supply chain expertise.
  • Power. Private participation in electricity distribution is due by the end of 2027, which will need engineers, operators and investors.

Five questions to ask next

  1. Will any Chinese support come with local content and training requirements for Ghanaian workers?
  2. Who monitors environmental standards on joint mining and processing projects?
  3. Will the planned procurement rules favouring local rice, textiles and medicines apply to Chinese and other foreign partners?
  4. How could foreign investors take part in the planned private participation in electricity distribution?
  5. Where do diaspora professionals plug in, and who is the point of contact?

For now, the takeaway is simple. Ghana has a plan, China has an offer, and the gap between the two is where the real story will be written.
